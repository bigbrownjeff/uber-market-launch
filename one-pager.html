The problem

A marketplace launch lands in the board deck as a growth story. Underneath, it's a labour-supply elasticity problem against a finite courier fleet — and the demand team and the supply team are paid against opposite KPIs: onboarding velocity vs. cheap incremental trips. Every merchant onboarded faster than you can fill at peak is a dollar pulled out of the incentive budget. When the courier-acceptance numbers come in softer than the deck promised, that seam is almost always where it broke.

What it is

Four artefacts, fit to your data:

  • A courier-elasticity model — your peak-window $/hr mapped to a fitted, diminishing-returns utilisation curve, per vertical (the food curve doesn't transfer to retail).
  • A free-rider decomposition — every dollar of lift split into couriers you bought vs. couriers who'd have shown up anyway.
  • A merchant cohort-decay panel — the survival cliff ("5 orders by week 4") that separates merchants who stick (69% still ordering at week 12, synthetic) from merchants who quietly fizzle (1%).
  • A joint scorecard — one weekly artefact your supply and demand analysts can both defend, and a cadence that forces the tradeoff into one room.

The wedge — vs. the demand-led launch playbook

The demand-led playbook isn't wrong — merchant onboarding velocity is a real lever, and it gets the press release. But the supply side gets the late-night Slack thread when Tuesday fill-rate drops below the merchant SLA. You win launches on the supply curve — so we instrument it first, and the free-rider column from day one, not the week of the budget review.

The free-rider metric is the one most experimental-design write-ups skip — and the first thing finance asks: what fraction of these couriers would have shown up anyway? In the simulator's synthetic baseline, a $5/hr peak incentive reads ~38% free riders and $10.45 per incremental trip — not the flattering per-trip number. Having that column ready is the difference between defending the program and watching it get cut.
